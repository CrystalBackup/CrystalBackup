A soak is fourteen days of not looking. Whatever the instrument gets wrong on the first morning is what you discover on the last one, with a fortnight of the wrong answer behind it and no way to re-run the two weeks. That is the whole argument for spending a release on the instrument before spending a fortnight on the thing it measures: a collector nobody has checked is worse than no collector, because it produces an archive that looks like evidence.

0.6.2 ships that instrument — a collector that samples the cluster on an interval and packages what it saw into an archive readable without access to the cluster — and then does the thing the fortnight cannot do for itself: points it at a cluster where the answers are already known. Its first four-hour run failed that comparison, in the most legible way available. It reported that it had measured nothing.

What follows below is the same 82 checks as the 0.6.1 report, run again on a fresh cluster for a different reason: the mover Jobs those checks create are now stamped with an identity label they did not carry for six milestones, and something is finally reading it. 82 of 82, no label filter, 1h53m17s.

What this run caught

The collector reported NOT_MEASURED beside dozens of real backups

The first four-hour run wrote, for the mover sizing classes data and manifests, the verdict NOT_MEASURED with its reason spelled out in full: “no data mover pod ran while the collector was up”. On a cluster where a campaign had just executed dozens of backups. Every word of that sentence is true about what the collector saw and false about the cluster — which is the failure mode a passive instrument has, and the reason this release exists at all. There were two independent causes, either one of which would have been enough on its own.

One: the label was never stamped. mover.BuildJob asserted no identity of its own — it copied the caller’s label map verbatim and added nothing to it — and 6 of the 10 sites that create a mover Job never put app.kubernetes.io/name=crystal-mover in that map. Both data movers, and all four manifest movers: precisely the two classes that came back NOT_MEASURED. Nothing in the operator selects on that label, so nothing malfunctioned, no reconcile went wrong and no test failed. It was simply not true in-cluster, and stayed not true for six milestones, because the first thing that ever tried to read it was this collector.

Two: the pods do not exist long enough to be polled. A mover Job is deleted by the Backup controller on the same reconcile pass that reads its result, so ttlSecondsAfterFinished never gets the chance to fire. Measured at 0.25s resolution, the four Jobs of a single ClusterBackup were visible for 9.6s, 11.0s, 16.3s and 23.7s — against a 15s sample interval. A poller misses most of those by construction; a poller that also filters on a label nobody stamps misses all of them, always.

Both are fixed, and deliberately not by fixing only one. BuildJob now stamps the identity label itself, rather than trusting ten call sites to each remember. The collector no longer depends on that label at all: it lists on managed-by and decides whether a Job is a mover from the --operation flag on its command line, so a creation site added in some future milestone is counted whether or not its author remembers the convention. And the exact figures now come from a watch rather than a poll — the poll stays, for the quantities that are genuinely sampled, and as the periodic re-list that repairs a watch that has silently dropped.

The first measured answer

What a mover actually used

0.6.1 shipped four sizing classes and said in as many words that they were defensible guesses with an override mechanism attached. This is the first run in which a guess was compared against anything. The figures come from the movers’ own termination messages — each pod reporting its own peak, rather than a sampler hoping to catch it at the right moment, which matters given how long these pods turn out to live.

Class Operations Peak observed Memory limit Share of limit
data backup, restore 81Mi 4Gi 2.0%
repo-heavy prune, check, sync 74Mi 8Gi 0.9%
repo-light init, forget, snapshots, unlock 101Mi 1Gi 9.9%
manifests the four manifest operations 105Mi 2Gi 5.1%

Zero OOM kills, zero evictions, zero limit hits across the campaign, in all four classes. This is the first time this project has been able to say that from measurement rather than from the absence of a complaint.

Now read the caveat, because it is larger than the result. This is a small repository. Every figure above was produced against seeded tenant volumes, on one cluster, in under two hours. The ordering it yields is the tell: repo-heavy came out lowest in absolute terms while carrying the highest ceiling — and that is not a finding about prune and check, it is what happens when the repository they are sized for barely exists. Replacing this table with numbers taken against real data is exactly what the fortnight is for.

So nothing on this page is an argument for lowering a ceiling. One measurement, on one repository, of operations whose cost scales with what they are pointed at, is an argument for keeping headroom — and for finally having an instrument that will say so when the headroom turns out to be wrong.

Also fixed

Three defects in the harness rather than the product

None of these could corrupt a backup. All three could make a fortnight of unattended collection worthless, or make a clean cluster look dirty — which, on the eve of a soak, is the same problem wearing different clothes.

crucible

The leak check called the instrument residue

Residue was matched by domain prefix — any object bearing a crystalbackup.io/* label in the operator’s namespace. The soak collector’s own PVC is such an object. Before the fix, seven checks each waited out their entire ten-minute budget, one after another, on a cluster carrying zero actual residue.

soak kit

collect.sh died before exporting

An unbraced expansion with an ellipsis glued straight onto it. Under a non-UTF-8 locale the script died on that line — after collecting, before writing the archive, which is the single point in the run where dying costs you everything you gathered.

soak kit

Its leak check failed every correct archive

The kit scans its own archive for un-redacted names before you send it anywhere. default is a namespace name; it is also an ordinary English word, one the kit’s own prose uses. So a perfectly redacted archive failed, every time, on the kit’s own explanatory text.

What this report does not say

The soak has not been run. This release is the instrument.

v0.6.2 is offered for testing in real conditions by early adopters, not for production. Everything below is still true after 82 green checks, and it would be easy to read this page as claiming otherwise:

  • The two-week soak alongside an incumbent tool has not happened. Neither has the pilot rollout. Both are M6’s own exit criteria and both remain unmet. What changed in 0.6.2 is that the soak now has something to watch it with — and that a four-hour rehearsal found that instrument reporting the opposite of the truth, which is a reasonable estimate of what a fortnight would have cost.
  • The sizing figures are one measurement on a small repository. They are the first this project has ever had, and they are not a load test. Nothing here speaks to a repository with millions of files, and the class whose cost tracks repository size is precisely the one this campaign could not stress.
  • Four hours of a collector is not fourteen days of a collector. The failure this release fixes was found in the first four hours; the failures a fortnight finds are the ones that need a fortnight — a watch that drops and is never re-established, an archive that grows without bound, a sampler whose clock drifts. The periodic re-list exists because that class of defect is expected, not because it has been observed and handled.
  • Six milestones of an unstamped label is a statement about coverage, not about labels. Nothing selected on app.kubernetes.io/name=crystal-mover, so nothing could fail when it was absent. Every convention no consumer reads is in the same position right now, and this suite would not catch those either.
  • A green run is a sample, not a proof. Timing-dependent defects in this project have historically appeared roughly one run in three, which is why M4 was accepted on seven independent lanes rather than one. This is a single session.

Run it on a cluster whose loss you can absorb, alongside — not instead of — whatever you back up with today. If you are running the soak kit, the numbers it collects are the point of the exercise: they are the ones meant to replace the table above.
